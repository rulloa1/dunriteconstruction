How to Run a Toolbox Talk

A toolbox talk is a short, focused safety conversation held at the jobsite — usually at the start of a shift or a new task. It keeps safety top of mind and gives the crew a chance to raise concerns. Each talk in this library is designed to take about five minutes.

Running the Talk

  1. Pick the talk that fits the day's work and hazards.
  2. Gather the crew where everyone can hear you. Keep it short and conversational.
  3. Read the key points, then connect them to today's specific tasks and site conditions.
  4. Ask the discussion questions — get the crew talking, not just listening.
  5. Answer questions and address concerns; fix any hazards raised.
  6. Have everyone sign the attendance sheet, and keep it on file.
Why it matters: Documented toolbox talks reinforce training, show your safety program is active, and are valuable records for OSHA, insurers, and project prequalification. More importantly — they prevent injuries.

Heat Illness Prevention

Why it matters: Florida heat and humidity make heat illness a year-round killer. Heat stroke can come on fast and is a medical emergency. Most heat illness is preventable with water, rest, and shade.

Key Points

  • Water — drink small amounts often, about a cup every 15–20 minutes; don't wait until you're thirsty.
  • Rest — take breaks in shade; increase break frequency as the heat index rises.
  • Shade — use shaded or cooled areas; never sit in a hot vehicle to rest.
  • Acclimatize — new and returning workers need 1–2 weeks to build tolerance; ease them in.
  • Watch each other — headache, dizziness, cramps, nausea, and heavy sweating are early warnings.

Emergency Signs — Call 911

Confusion, slurred speech, fainting, seizures, or hot/dry skin signal heat stroke. Call 911, move the person to shade, and cool them aggressively with water and ice while waiting.

Discussion

  • Where's our water and shade on this site today?
  • Who's new or just back, and needs to take it easier today?
  • What's the heat index, and how will we adjust breaks?

Fatal Four

Fall Protection

Why it matters: Falls are the #1 killer in construction. Most fall deaths happen at heights people think are "no big deal." Six feet onto rebar or concrete is enough to kill.

Key Points

  • Fall protection is required at 6 feet or more — guardrails, covers, or a personal fall arrest system (PFAS).
  • Inspect your harness, lanyard, and connectors before every use; remove damaged gear from service.
  • Tie off to a proper anchor (capable of 5,000 lbs per worker or engineered); keep your tie-off point high to limit fall distance.
  • Cover and mark every floor and roof opening — don't step on or move a cover.
  • 100% tie-off when required: stay connected while moving.

Discussion

  • Where are the fall hazards on this site today?
  • What are we tying off to, and is it adequate?
  • Are all openings and edges protected?

Ladder Safety

Why it matters: Ladder falls send thousands of construction workers to the ER every year. Most are caused by the wrong ladder, a bad setup, or rushing.

Key Points

  • Inspect before use; tag and remove damaged ladders.
  • Extension ladders: 4:1 ratio (1 ft out per 4 ft up) and extend 3 ft above the landing.
  • Maintain three points of contact and face the ladder when climbing.
  • Secure the ladder; don't overreach — move it instead.
  • Never stand on the top cap or top rung of a stepladder; use the right duty rating.
  • Keep metal ladders away from electrical hazards.

Discussion

  • Is this the right ladder for the job, or are we making do?
  • Is it set at the right angle and secured?
  • Could a lift or scaffold be safer here?

04
Excavation

Trenching & Excavation

Why it matters: A cubic yard of soil weighs as much as a car. A trench collapse buries and kills in seconds — there's no time to react. No worker enters an unprotected trench, ever.

Key Points

  • Trenches 5 ft+ need a protective system: sloping, benching, shoring, or a trench box.
  • Always call 811 before digging to locate underground utilities.
  • A competent person inspects daily, before each shift, and after rain.
  • Keep spoil piles and equipment back at least 2 ft from the edge.
  • Have a ladder or ramp within 25 ft of workers in trenches 4 ft+.
  • Watch for water buildup and hazardous atmospheres.

Discussion

  • Have utilities been located and marked?
  • What's our protective system and who's the competent person?
  • How do we get out fast if we need to?

05
Fatal Four

Electrical Safety & Power Lines

Why it matters: Electricity is one of the Fatal Four. Contact with overhead power lines and damaged cords kills construction workers every year — and you don't get a second chance.

Key Points

  • Assume every line and wire is energized until proven otherwise.
  • Stay at least 10 ft from overhead power lines with equipment, ladders, and materials.
  • Use GFCI protection on all 120V construction receptacles.
  • Inspect cords and tools; remove those with damage or missing ground pins.
  • Keep panels accessible and covered; protect temporary wiring from damage and water.
  • Only qualified persons work on energized equipment — lock out where possible.

Discussion

  • Where are the overhead lines, and what's our clearance plan?
  • Are our cords and tools in good shape?
  • Is GFCI protection in place?

06
Health Hazard

Silica Dust

Why it matters: Cutting, grinding, and drilling concrete and masonry releases invisible silica dust. Breathing it causes silicosis and lung cancer — diseases that show up years later and can't be cured.

Key Points

  • Control the dust at the source: use water (wet cutting) or vacuum dust collection with a HEPA filter.
  • Follow the Table 1 method for your task; wear the required respirator when called for.
  • Never dry sweep silica dust or clean it with compressed air.
  • Keep others out of the dust zone.
  • If you're required to wear a respirator 30+ days/year for silica, medical surveillance is offered.

Discussion

  • Which tasks today create silica dust?
  • Do we have water or vacuum controls ready?
  • Who needs a respirator, and is it fit-tested?

07
PPE

PPE: Wear It Right

Why it matters: PPE is your last line of defense. It only works if you wear it, wear it correctly, and keep it in good shape. The few seconds it takes to put it on can save your sight, hearing, or life.

Key Points

  • Always on site: hard hat, safety glasses, high-visibility apparel, sturdy boots.
  • Match PPE to the hazard — gloves, face shield, hearing protection, respirator.
  • Inspect PPE before use; replace anything cracked, frayed, or worn out.
  • Fit matters: a loose harness or wrong-size respirator won't protect you.
  • Keep safety glasses clean and on — most eye injuries happen to people "just doing one quick thing."

Discussion

  • What PPE does today's work require beyond the basics?
  • Is anyone missing or short on gear?
  • Is everyone's gear in good condition?

08
Walking-Working Surfaces

Housekeeping & Slips, Trips, Falls

Why it matters: Slips, trips, and falls on the same level cause a huge share of jobsite injuries — sprains, broken bones, and worse when near edges or openings. A clean site is a safe site.

Key Points

  • Clean as you go — don't let debris, cords, and materials pile up.
  • Keep walkways, stairs, and exits clear.
  • Route and cover cords and hoses so they're not trip hazards.
  • Clean up spills and mud immediately; mark wet areas.
  • Stack and store materials safely so they can't fall or block paths.
  • Watch footing in Florida rain and on uneven ground.

Discussion

  • Where are the trip hazards on this site right now?
  • Whose job is cleanup for today's tasks?
  • Are paths and exits clear?

09
Tools & Equipment

Power Tools & Equipment

Why it matters: Power tools cause serious cuts, amputations, and eye injuries — usually from missing guards, dull blades, or rushing. Respect the tool and it works with you, not against you.

Key Points

  • Use only tools you're trained and authorized to operate.
  • Inspect before use; never use a tool with a missing or disabled guard.
  • Use the right tool for the job; keep blades and bits sharp and correct.
  • Wear required eye and hearing protection; secure loose clothing and jewelry.
  • Disconnect power before changing blades/bits or clearing jams.
  • Keep hands clear of the cut line; let the tool stop before setting it down.

Discussion

  • Are all guards in place on today's tools?
  • Is anyone using a tool they haven't been trained on?
  • What PPE does each tool require?

10
Fatal Four

Struck-By & Caught-Between

Why it matters: Struck-by and caught-between hazards — vehicles, equipment, swinging loads, falling objects — are two of the Fatal Four. Stay visible, stay clear, and watch the heavy iron.

Key Points

  • Wear high-visibility apparel and make eye contact with equipment operators.
  • Stay out of the swing radius and never stand under a suspended load.
  • Watch for backing vehicles; use spotters and stay out of blind spots.
  • Secure tools and materials at heights so nothing falls on the crew below.
  • Keep clear of pinch points between equipment, walls, and materials.
  • Never position yourself between moving equipment and a fixed object.

Discussion

  • Where's the equipment working today, and where are the no-go zones?
  • Do we need spotters or a traffic plan?
  • What could fall, and who's below?

11
Ergonomics

Manual Lifting & Back Safety

Why it matters: Back and shoulder injuries from lifting are some of the most common — and most preventable — injuries in construction. They can end a career. Lift smart, not hard.

Key Points

  • Size it up first — test the weight; if it's too heavy or awkward, get help or a machine.
  • Lift with your legs, not your back — bend the knees, keep the load close, back straight.
  • Don't twist while lifting — turn with your feet.
  • Use carts, dollies, and team lifts for heavy or bulky loads.
  • Keep clear paths and good footing before you carry.
  • Take breaks on repetitive lifting; warm up.

Discussion

  • What heavy or awkward loads are we moving today?
  • What equipment could do the lifting for us?
  • When do we call for a team lift?

12
Environmental · Florida Priority

Severe Weather & Lightning

Why it matters: Florida is the lightning capital of the U.S., and storms blow up fast. Lightning, high winds, and flooding can turn a normal day dangerous in minutes. Know the plan before the sky turns.

Key Points

  • Lightning 30/30 rule: take shelter when thunder follows lightning by 30 seconds or less; wait 30 minutes after the last thunder to resume.
  • Get to a safe shelter — a building or enclosed vehicle — not under trees, scaffolds, or equipment.
  • Stop crane, lift, and elevated work when storms approach.
  • Secure loose materials and tools before high winds.
  • Avoid standing water — it can hide electrical and trip hazards.
  • Know the site's shelter location and the hurricane plan (Handbook §6).

Discussion

  • What's today's forecast, and who's watching the sky?
  • Where do we shelter, and how do we call the stop?
  • What needs securing if wind picks up?
